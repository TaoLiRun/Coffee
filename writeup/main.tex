\title{\LARGE What Happens When Purchase Intentions Are Blocked: \\Evidence from a Large Coffee Chain\footnote{This project is funded by Hong Kong RGC Grant \#16506525}}

\author{\bfseries Francesco Gabriele\thanks{Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Business School, Department of Marketing.} \quad Tao Li \footnotemark[2]  \quad Chen Lin\thanks{Fudan University, Department of Marketing.} \quad Mengze Shi \footnotemark[2] \quad\thanks{Authors are ordered alphabetically}}
\date{Draft Version: \today} 
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\indexspace

\noindent Temporary disruption of planned choices can affect consumers' choices in the long term. We study its impact leveraging 18 temporary store closures at a large coffee chain during the Covid-19 pandemic. A triple-differences design compares consumers' post-reopening changes in choices for those who are more and less likely to purchase during the closure. We find that purchase frequency rises 0.58 percentage points more for consumers who are more to purchase and thus faced a disruption, while novelty-seeking, which is measured by the share of distinct purchased products not previously chosen by the consumer, drops 4.15 percentage points for them. This novelty differential identifies the incremental response to interrupting a likely purchase as a magnitude equal to 8.3\% of pre-period mean of novelty-seeking. We propose counterfactual thinking and goal persistence as the mechanism under this finding: a blocked purchase intention triggers consumers to think about their previous choices, raising the prominence of products they purchased before. Our heterogeneity analysis demonstrate a choice-polarization effect: the drop in novelty-seeking is even larger for consumers with a lower novelty-seeking level. This supports our mechanism because counterfactual thinking about the previous choices is more likely to happen to consumers with a lower novelty-seeking level.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{section:1}

\noindent Consumers sometimes face a temporary store closure when they are likely to purchase. The natural benchmark is that such a disruption shifts demand over time: consumers delay purchases and resume their usual behavior once access returns. We study whether this benchmark fails by distinguishing episodes with high and low predicted purchase incidence during the closure. When a closure interrupts a likely purchase, it may affect not only when consumers buy, but also how they choose once they return.

This distinction raises two central questions. Do interruptions to likely purchases reduce subsequent purchase frequency, or do consumers return to their prior activity? Does the post-reopening product-choice response differ between high- and low-predicted-incidence episodes? These questions determine whether temporary constraints merely reallocate demand across time or also reshape consumer choice.

We address these questions using panel data from a large digital coffee retailer. The data cover 779,744 consumers and 260 stores from June 1, 2020 through December 31, 2021. Temporary store closures during the Covid-19 pandemic interrupt access to specific locations but are not targeted to individual consumers. They therefore create sharp, localized disruptions that allow us to track how the same consumers adjust after access returns.

A central challenge is that the closure does not bind equally for all consumers. We use pre-disruption purchase behavior to construct an ex-ante prediction of purchase incidence during the closure window. We then estimate a triple-differences design that compares (i) treated versus control consumers, (ii) before versus after reopening, and (iii) high versus low predicted purchase incidence. The resulting high-minus-low contrast isolates the incremental response associated with interrupting a likely purchase only under the identifying assumptions stated below; it is not the total treatment effect for high-predicted-incidence episodes.

Our analysis yields two main results. First, purchase frequency rises by 0.67 percentage points per calendar day for high-predicted-incidence episodes and by 0.09 points for low-predicted-incidence episodes. The high-minus-low DDD is therefore 0.58 points. Because its pretrend test rejects equality, we interpret this result as descriptive evidence of post-reopening recovery rather than a clean causal rebound.

Second, novelty-seeking---the share of distinct products in the basket that the consumer has not purchased before---rises by 3.13 percentage points for low-predicted-incidence episodes. The corresponding high-group point estimate is a 1.02-point decline. Their difference is a $-4.15$-point DDD, or 8.3\% of the 49.78\% pre-period novelty mean. Economically, the interruption more than offsets the low group's increase in exploration and leaves the high group's product-choice response slightly negative.

As a robustness check on the outcome definition, the appendix replaces novelty-seeking with new-product-seeking, which measures the share of purchased products that have appeared recently in the transaction data. The alternative measure produces the same qualitative conclusion; Appendix~\ref{app:new_product_seeking} reports the estimates and discusses their interpretation.

One interpretation is a goal-based mechanism. When a likely purchase is interrupted, the underlying goal may remain active. Upon reopening, consumers may prioritize goal completion and direct purchases toward familiar products. Consistent with this interpretation, the fitted novelty DDD is $-4.24$ points for a consumer one standard deviation below mean baseline novelty, $-1.30$ points at the mean and $+1.63$ points one standard deviation above the mean, holding baseline order history fixed. Thus, moving from one standard deviation below to one standard deviation above mean baseline novelty changes the fitted interruption response by 5.88 points. These fitted values summarize an exploratory continuous interaction rather than separately estimated subgroup effects.

Our paper contributes to the marketing literature along three dimensions. First, we study how temporary constraints interact with consumer intent to shape demand, providing evidence of state dependence that is consistent with, but does not uniquely identify, persistent purchase goals. Second, we contribute to the literature on supply-side shocks by showing that their effects depend on whether the constraint binds for the consumer, emphasizing the importance of heterogeneity in exposure to disruptions. The closest applied benchmark is \shortciteA{Levine2025}, who study a temporary grocery strike and use predicted trip displacement to distinguish baseline demand changes from the effect of an interrupted shopping trip. We use the same basic logic of separating exposure from binding interruption, but shift the object of interest from store choice and trip counts to product exploration within a changing digital assortment. Third, we speak to the literature on product discovery by showing how temporary interruptions can change exploration even when demand levels recover quickly. More broadly, our results suggest that understanding demand under temporary constraints requires going beyond average responses and explicitly accounting for how disruptions interact with consumer intent.

\section{Behavioral Mechanism and Micro-Foundation}

\subsection{Counterfactual Thinking and Goal Persistence}

A growing literature in marketing and consumer behavior studies how consumers respond when intended purchases cannot be completed due to temporary constraints such as stockouts, unavailable products, or interruptions in access. A natural consequence is that these disruptions primarily shift purchases across time, with consumers delaying consumption and resuming their prior behavior once constraints are lifted. Under this view, temporary supply-side disruptions affect the timing of purchases but do not fundamentally alter the underlying decision process. However, prior research in consumer psychology suggests that interrupted actions can generate persistent cognitive and motivational effects that extend beyond simple postponement. In our case, when a store closure blocks an intended purchase, the underlying goal need not disappear. Instead, the interruption may leave the goal cognitively active, increasing its salience and shaping subsequent behavior once purchasing opportunities are restored.

In this section, we conceptualize a mechanism based on counterfactual thinking and the persistence of active purchase goals, and then form predictions about our empirical results based on the mechanism.
Our idea comes from a large literature in psychology showing that unfulfilled goals trigger counterfactual thinking about the missed action, remain mentally active, and intensify the desire to complete the interrupted task \shortcite<e.g.,>{Brehm1966, Clee1980, fitzsimons2000consumer,epstude2008functional,masicampo2011consider,Kappes2016}.

Counterfactual thinking refers to the mental simulation of how events could have unfolded differently under alternative circumstances \shortcite{epstude2008functional}. Importantly, this literature argues that counterfactual thoughts are not merely retrospective evaluations, but functional cognitive processes that shape future behavior by maintaining attention on unrealized goals and missed actions. When individuals are prevented from completing an intended action, they naturally construct upward counterfactuals such as ``I would have purchased if the store had remained open.'' These mental simulations increase the salience of the interrupted goal and motivate subsequent goal-completion behavior.

This perspective is particularly relevant in our setting. Temporary store closures do not simply eliminate purchasing opportunities at random. For consumers with an active purchase intention, the closure interrupts an ongoing consumption objective. As a result, the disruption creates a counterfactual consumption episode in which consumers mentally represent the purchase they would have made absent the closure. We argue that this counterfactual process generates a form of goal persistence that shapes post-reopening behavior.

We argue that counterfactual thinking represents the cognitive mechanism through which interrupted purchase intentions remain behaviorally active. When consumers are prevented from completing an intended purchase, they mentally simulate the forgone consumption episode. This counterfactual representation maintains the original consumption goal in an active state, leading consumers to return after reopening with more focused and less exploratory purchase behavior.

Our mechanism is closely related to research on interrupted goals and implemental mindsets in consumer behavior. Prior work shows that unfulfilled goals remain cognitively active and continue to influence subsequent decisions until they are completed \shortcite<e.g.>{gollwitzer1990action, kruglanski2002theory, masicampo2011consider}. Similarly, research on shopping momentum demonstrates that consumers who enter an implemental mindset become more focused on goal completion and less deliberative in their subsequent choices \shortcite{dhar2007shopping}. In our context, consumers whose purchases are blocked by store closures may remain in a suspended implemental state during the disruption period. Once stores reopen, these consumers return with a more focused purchase objective, prioritizing completion of the interrupted consumption goal rather than exploring alternative products.

This mechanism also relates to the literature on stockouts and constrained choice. \shortciteA{fitzsimons2000consumer} show that consumers’ responses to unavailable products depend critically on the strength of their commitment to the intended purchase. When commitment is high, constraints generate stronger behavioral reactions and alter subsequent decision-making. Our setting extends this insight by considering temporary store closures that interrupt not only access to a specific product, but the entire execution of a planned consumption episode. Rather than weakening demand, these interruptions may preserve or even intensify purchase intentions through counterfactual processing.

A recent cognitive theory of reasoning and choice provides useful language for this channel. \shortciteA{bordalo2026cognitive} argue that decision makers first recognize the kind of problem they face using contextual cues and past experiences, and that this categorization determines which attributes receive attention. Applied to our setting, a closure can change the post-reopening problem from ordinary exploration in a coffee assortment to completion of an interrupted purchase routine. The mechanism therefore does not require consumers' tastes or information to change. Instead, the same consumer may place more attention on familiar products because the closure makes the familiar consumption path the natural category through which the next purchase is represented.

The implications of this mechanism are central to our empirical findings. If consumers whose purchase intention is blocked engage in counterfactual thinking and maintain an active purchase goal, they may return once constraints are lifted and complete the intended consumption activity. Because substitution or discouragement may offset this response, however, the framework does not impose a signed prediction on subsequent purchase frequency. At the same time, because post-reopening behavior is driven by goal completion, consumers should become less likely to deviate from the originally intended purchase path. Consequently, consumers whose purchase intention is blocked should exhibit lower product exploration and reduced novelty-seeking after reopening.

\subsection{Missed Exposure and Product Discovery}

In addition to goal persistence, we consider a complementary informational mechanism based on missed exposure to new products. Product discovery in retail environments often emerges through repeated interaction with evolving assortments, browsing behavior, and incidental exposure to available alternatives. Consumers do not necessarily discover new products through active search alone; rather, repeated marketplace interactions shape awareness, consideration sets, and subsequent experimentation decisions. As a result, temporary disruptions in marketplace access may reduce not only purchasing opportunities, but also consumers’ exposure to newly introduced products and evolving assortments.

This mechanism is closely related to the literature on variety-seeking and exploratory consumption. Prior research shows that experimentation depends not only on intrinsic preferences for novelty, but also on the structure of the choice environment and the set of alternatives consumers are exposed to \shortcite<e.g.>{mcalister1982variety, kahn1995consumer, vantrijp1996variety}. In particular, variety-seeking often arises through repeated exposure to changing alternatives, which stimulates curiosity, learning, and experimentation. Consumers who interact more frequently with a retail environment accumulate broader knowledge of the assortment and become more likely to adopt newly introduced products over time. A related literature on consideration sets and consumer learning further suggests that product adoption critically depends on awareness and exposure. Consumers can only evaluate and adopt products that enter their consideration set, and marketplace interactions play an important role in determining which products become cognitively accessible \shortcite{hauser1990consideration, nedungadi1990consideration}. Temporary interruptions in store access may therefore reduce the likelihood that newly introduced products enter consumers’ active choice sets. Even when consumers return after the disruption, they may remain less informed about assortment changes that occurred during the closure period.

Our setting provides a natural environment in which this mechanism may operate. Product assortments evolve continuously across stores and over time, with substantial heterogeneity in product availability and product introductions. Consumers who are unable to access stores during closure periods miss repeated opportunities to observe new offerings, promotional displays, and assortment updates. Relative to continuously exposed consumers, blocked consumers may therefore return with lower awareness of newly introduced items and weaker familiarity with the updated assortment. This reduction in exposure may subsequently lower the probability of novelty adoption after reopening.


Importantly, the missed-exposure mechanism generates predictions that are conceptually distinct from the counterfactual-goal mechanism. The counterfactual-goal mechanism predicts that blocked consumers return with a more focused and goal-directed consumption process. Because the interrupted purchase intention remains cognitively active, consumers prioritize completion of the originally intended consumption objective and become less likely to deviate toward exploratory purchases. Under this mechanism, reduced experimentation reflects motivated goal completion.

In contrast, the missed-exposure mechanism operates through informational frictions and differences in product awareness. Consumers may become less exploratory not because they are more focused on completing a prior goal, but because they are less informed about newly available products. The mechanism therefore reflects a reduction in assortment awareness rather than a change in motivational state. Distinguishing between these explanations is important because they imply different behavioral foundations for the observed reduction in novelty-seeking.

Our empirical analysis evaluates the relative importance of these channels by combining plausibly external variation in store access with heterogeneity in ex-ante purchase intention. In particular, we exploit cross-event variation in the intensity of new product introductions during closure periods to test whether closures associated with greater missed exposure generate stronger reductions in subsequent exploration. This approach allows us to distinguish whether post-reopening behavior is primarily driven by persistent counterfactual purchase goals or by informational differences in exposure to new products.

\subsection{A Behavioral Micro-Foundation}
\label{section:2a} 

Consider a consumer who visits a coffee store and chooses between familiar products and new products. Familiar products are items the consumer has purchased before, while new products are items the consumer has not yet tried. In a standard choice framework, the probability of choosing a new product depends on intrinsic preferences, variety-seeking motives, and idiosyncratic taste shocks.

Our key departure is that a temporary closure can alter the consumer’s attentional state. For consumers with an active purchase intention, the closure does not simply remove a transaction opportunity. It interrupts an intended consumption episode. This interruption induces counterfactual thinking: the consumer mentally represents the purchase she would have made had the store remained open. The counterfactual does not necessarily make one specific product more attractive. Instead, it reactivates the consumer’s familiar consumption routine.

This distinction is important. In a coffee setting, the interrupted goal is often not ``buy product $j$'' in a narrow sense, but rather ``complete my usual coffee purchase.'' The relevant object of salience is therefore the familiar consumption path: the set of products, routines, and choices that the consumer has previously experienced. Counterfactual thinking increases the cognitive accessibility of this familiar path. When the store reopens, the consumer returns with a more focused consumption objective, placing greater attentional weight on familiar products and less relative attention on unfamiliar alternatives.

The model can be summarized in three steps.

First, before the closure, the consumer has a familiar consumption routine formed through past purchases. This routine defines a set of familiar products that are cognitively accessible and easy to choose. New products may still be attractive, but they require attention, discovery, and willingness to deviate from the routine.

Second, the closure blocks the consumer’s intended purchase. For consumers who would have purchased during the closure period, this generates a counterfactual representation: ``I would have bought my usual coffee if the store had been open.'' This representation keeps the familiar routine active during the disruption.

Third, after reopening, the consumer makes a product choice under distorted attention. Familiar products receive an additional salience weight because they are linked to the interrupted consumption goal. New products do not receive this salience boost because they were not part of the counterfactual consumption script. As a result, the probability of choosing a familiar product rises, and the probability of novelty-seeking falls.

The framework establish formal predictions about the impact on novelty-seeking. First, at the population level, blocked intention should reduce novelty-seeking because counterfactual thinking increases the salience of old products. Second, this reduction should be stronger for low-novelty consumers, widening the novelty gap between high- and low-novelty types.

This framework clarifies the behavioral distinction between counterfactual thinking and goal-focused behavior. Counterfactual thinking is the cognitive process generated by the interruption. It makes the unrealized consumption episode mentally accessible. Goal-focused behavior is the observable consequence: consumers return to complete the familiar consumption routine, reducing exploration.

It also distinguishes the counterfactual-goal mechanism from the missed-exposure mechanism. Under the counterfactual-goal mechanism, consumers are aware of alternatives but pay less attention to them because the familiar routine is salient. Under the missed-exposure mechanism, consumers fail to explore because new products are less likely to enter their consideration set. Both mechanisms predict lower novelty-seeking, but they operate through different primitives: salience and motivation in the first case, information and awareness in the second.


\paragraph{Mapping to notation.}
The discussion above implies a simple modeling restriction. When a consumer's intended coffee purchase is blocked, the familiar consumption path that would otherwise have been completed remains cognitively accessible after reopening. The model represents this carryover as greater relative attention to familiar products. It first derives the average novelty-seeking prediction and then allows the strength of this response to vary with baseline novelty-seeking.

\subsubsection{Setup}

Consider consumer $i$ and relative period $t$. Let $R_t\in\{0,1\}$ indicate the pre- and post-reopening period, and let $C_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether consumer $i$ is exposed to the closure. Let $p_{i}\in[0,1]$ denote the ex-ante probability that consumer $i$ would have made a purchase during the closure window absent the closure. This probability can be treated as a general summary of consumers' purchase frequency before the closure. It is therefore a continuous measure of the strength of the potentially interrupted shopping episode.

We model the choice between two mutually exclusive product categories, $a\in\{O,N\}$, conditions on a purchase, where $O$ denotes an old category consisting of products previously purchased by the consumer, and $N$ denotes a new category consisting of products the consumer has not previously purchased. Let $q_{it}$ denote the probability of choosing new products, i.e. $a=N$,
conditional on making a purchase.
Finally, let $\vartheta_i\in\mathbb R$ denote the consumer's intrinsic preference for novelty products. A higher value of $\vartheta_i$ represents a stronger baseline tendency to choose the new category.

\subsubsection{Counterfactual Thinking and Familiar-Routine Salience}

A temporary closure is an exceptional interruption of the consumer's normal shopping process. When the consumer was likely to shop during the closure window, she may subsequently imagine the shopping episode that would have occurred had the store remained open. Counterfactual thinking transforms this unrealized shopping opportunity into a mentally accessible choice episode and can thereby influence subsequent goal-directed behavior \shortcite{epstude2008functional}.

The expected intensity of this counterfactual representation is proportional to $R_tC_{i}p_{i}$, because the impact is only represented in the post-period, among consumers who are exposed to a closure and are accompanied with a higher likelihood of purchase.

Let $\omega(\vartheta_i)$ denote the excess cognitive accessibility of the old-product category relative to the new-product category within the counterfactual shopping episode. We claims two behavioral restrictions.

First, counterfactual thinking is anchored in the consumer's established historical routine, therefore makes the familiar products relatively more accessible:
\begin{equation}
    \omega(\vartheta_i)>0.
    \label{eq:micro_routine_anchoring}
\end{equation}

Second, familiar-routine anchoring becomes weaker as preference for novelty-seeking increases:
\begin{equation}
    \omega'(\vartheta_i)<0.
    \label{eq:micro_novelty_attenuation}
\end{equation}
A consumer with a stronger novelty-seeking preference has a less entrenched familiar-category default and generates a broader set of alternatives when imagining the forgone shopping episode. Her counterfactual is consequently less concentrated on the old category.

The closure doesn't changes the intrinsic quality or consumption utility of either category. Rather, counterfactual thinking changes their relative cognitive accessibility at the time of choice. By this, the difference in utility of choosing new versus old product can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    U^N_{it}-U^O_{it}
    =
    \overline{\Delta U}_{it}(\vartheta_i)
    -\kappa\omega(\vartheta_i)R_tC_{i}p_{i}
    +\varepsilon^N_{it}-\varepsilon^O_{it},
    \label{eq:micro_choice_index}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\Delta U}_{it}(\vartheta_i)$ is the baseline difference in the absence of closure-induced counterfactual salience, $\kappa>0$ indicates the extent of goal persistence, while $\varepsilon^N_{it}$ and $\varepsilon^O_{it}$ are the idiosyncratic errors.
Under a binary logit representation, equation~\eqref{eq:micro_choice_index} implies that the propensity of choosing new products can be represented as
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{it}
    \equiv
    \log\left(\frac{q_{it}}{1-q_{it}}\right)
    =
    \ell^0_{it}(\vartheta_i)
    -\lambda(\vartheta_i)R_tC_{i}p_{i},
    \label{eq:micro_log_odds}
\end{equation}
where $\ell^0_{it}(\vartheta_i)$ contains baseline preferences and all main and lower-order effects unrelated to the counterfactual channel and $\lambda(\vartheta_i) \equiv \kappa\omega(\vartheta_i)$ is the familiar-routine salience parameter.
The primitive restrictions $\kappa>0$, $\omega(\vartheta_i)>0$, and $\omega'(\vartheta_i)<0$ imply
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(\vartheta_i)>0
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    \lambda'(\vartheta_i)<0.
    \label{eq:micro_lambda_restrictions}
\end{equation}
Thus, the counterfactual-thinking mechanism generates familiar-routine salience that weakens with novelty preference.

To isolate the mechanism, let the benchmark new-product choice propensity satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}
    \Delta_C\Delta_R
    \ell^0_{iet}(\vartheta_i)
    =0.
    \label{eq:micro_baseline_ddd_restriction}
\end{equation}
This is the behavioral-model analogue of the empirical parallel-triple-trends restriction: absent the counterfactual channel, the post--pre treated--control contrast does not vary systematically with $p_{i}$.

\subsubsection{Homogeneous Benchmark}

First, hold novelty preference fixed and suppose that the familiar-routine salience parameter is common across consumers, $\lambda(\vartheta_i)=\overline{\lambda}>0$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:micro_log_odds} then becomes
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{it}
    =
    \ell^0_{it}
    -\overline{\lambda}R_tC_{i}p_{i}.
    \label{eq:micro_homogeneous}
\end{equation}

Let $\Delta_R$ denote the post-minus-pre difference and $\Delta_C$ the treated-minus-control difference. The incremental effect of counterfactual thinking on novelty-seeking behavior in the post-period can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    D_p
    \equiv
    \frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}
    \Delta_C\Delta_R\ell_{it}=-\overline{\lambda}<0.
    \label{eq:micro_continuous_ddd_definition}
\end{equation}

If we differentiate consumers of high purchase intention with those of low purchase intention, i.e. $p_H>p_L$, the corresponding effect is
\begin{align}
    D(p_H,p_L)
    &\equiv
    \Delta_C\Delta_R\ell(p_H)
    -\Delta_C\Delta_R\ell(p_L) \\
    &=-\overline{\lambda}(p_H-p_L)<0.
    \label{eq:micro_finite_ddd}
\end{align}

\begin{prediction}[Counterfactual interruption reduces new-category choice]
Since counterfactual thinking makes the familiar routine relatively salient, $\overline{\lambda}>0$, then the post--pre treated--control response in new-category choice becomes more negative as the probability of a forgone shopping episode increases:
\begin{equation}
    D_p<0.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, for $p_H>p_L$, the high-minus-low triple difference satisfies
\begin{equation}
    D(p_H,p_L)<0.
\end{equation}
\end{prediction}

\subsubsection{Continuous Heterogeneity in novelty preference}

Now allow novelty preference to vary continuously across consumers. Higher novelty preference has two conceptually distinct roles. First, it increases the baseline tendency to choose the new category, i.e. $\frac{\partial\ell^0_{it}(\vartheta_i)}{\partial\vartheta_i}>0$.
Second, it weakens the familiar-routine salience generated by counterfactual thinking, i.e. $\lambda'(\vartheta_i)<0$.

The interruption-related effect for a consumer with novelty preference $\vartheta_i$ is
\begin{equation}
    D_p(\vartheta_i)
    =
    \frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}
    \Delta_C\Delta_R\ell_{it}
    =-\lambda(\vartheta_i)<0.
    \label{eq:micro_type_specific_ddd}
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to novelty preference gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial D_p(\vartheta_i)}
    {\partial\vartheta_i}
    =-\lambda'(\vartheta_i)>0.
    \label{eq:micro_heterogeneity_derivative}
\end{equation}

\begin{prediction}[novelty preference attenuates familiar-routine salience]
The counterfactual interruption effect on new-category choice is less negative for consumers with a stronger novelty preference:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial D_p(\vartheta_i)}
    {\partial\vartheta_i}>0.
\end{equation}
This is an attenuation prediction. It does not require the interruption effect to become positive for highly novelty-oriented consumers.
\end{prediction}

\section{Setting and Data}
\label{section:2}   

\noindent We use administrative purchase records from a large Chinese quick-service coffee chain whose customers order primarily through a mobile application. The data cover June 1, 2020 through December 31, 2021---19 calendar months---and contain 7,312,498 orders, represented by 10,631,250 product-line records, from 779,744 customers at 260 stores in a major city. We observe the customer, purchase date, store and product for each order.

Temporary store closures due to Covid-19 lock down create the treatment variation. We identify store closures of stores with no transactions in a consecutive days, leading to 18 identified closures. The detailed applied sample screens are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:closure_registry}. The temporary closures happened mostly in July and August, 2021, because of a large round of lock down in the city, varying from 10 to 30 days. A store closure can potentially affect all consumers in our record, as consumers can purchase in any store. We first restrict our attention to consumers with at least 5 purchases before the closures happened, and this constitutes around 30\% of our sample. We assign treatment status to consumers who have a focal store and that store experienced a closure. Specifically, the focal store of a consumer is defined as the one where the consumer made at least 80\% of purchases among his all purchases. For each store closure identified, we select 5 similar stores (e.g. have a similar setup date) that operate normally through all time. We treat the consumers whose focal stores are among these selected control stores as control customers. These restrictions make the focal store a meaningful source of treatment exposure.

The resulting sample contains 18 closures and 40,148 customers in total, including 7,390 treated customers and 32,758 controls customers. For each temporary closure, we consider four pre-closure and four post-reopening windows, with time window length the same as the closure length. 

\subsection{Classifying Predicted Purchase Incidence}
\label{section:classifier}
The central challenge in this setting is that not all treated customers are equally affected by a store closure. While all customers assigned to a treated store are exposed to the disruption, only a subset would have made a purchase during the closure window absent the interruption. We separate closure exposure from likely purchase interruption by classifying member-events according to predicted purchase incidence. A likely interrupted purchase occurs only when a high-predicted-incidence episode is also treated. The score is a prediction of purchase timing, not a direct measure of a psychological purchase intention.

We classify each member-event pair with a predictive XGBoost model trained on pre-closure behavior. The model estimates whether the member would make at least one chain purchase in a window with the same length as the closure. We train one model for each closure duration, since the probability of at least one purchase mechanically depends on the number of days in the prediction window. Training uses relative periods $-4$ through $-2$, validation uses the holdout pre-closure period $-1$, and an additional audit evaluates control customers in period $0$, when their matched stores remain open. For treated customers, the corresponding period-0 target absent the focal-store closure is counterfactual, so we use the predicted probability. We classify a member-event as high predicted purchase incidence when this probability is at least 0.5.

The features use only information available before the prediction window: recent purchase frequency, recency since the last purchase, total pre-period purchase days, store loyalty, basket composition, discount and coupon use, day-of-week purchasing patterns, demographics and push-notification history.

\section{Empirical Strategy and Results}

\subsection{Outcomes}
\label{section:3.1}  

\noindent Let $i$ index consumers, $e$ closure events, $t$ time, and let $L_e$ denote the duration in days of closure event $e$. For each event, we construct relative periods $t=-4,\ldots,4$, where each period is a window of length $L_e$ and period 0 is the closure window. The main regressions omit period 0 and compare four pre-closure windows with four post-reopening windows. This construction produces a member-event-period panel in which each member-event contributes up to eight observations to the purchase-frequency analysis. We define two main outcomes on these windows.

\paragraph{Purchase frequency.}
We measure purchase intensity using purchase frequency, defined as the number of days with at least one purchase at any chain store per calendar day within each event-length window. For consumer $i$ and event $e$, we define purchase frequency as
\begin{equation}
	\text{Y}^P_{iet} = \frac{1}{L_e} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{W}_e} \mathbb{1}\{ \text{Purchase}_{it} = 1 \},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{W}_e$ denotes the set of days in the relevant window pre- or post-closure and $\mathbb{1}\{\cdot\}$ is an indicator equal to one if consumer $i$ makes at least one chain purchase on day $t$. Multiple orders on the same day count once. Normalizing by closure length makes purchase frequency comparable across closure events of different durations.

\paragraph{Novelty-seeking.}
To measure exploration relative to a consumer's own purchase history, let $\mathcal{J}_{iet}$ denote the set of distinct products that consumer $i$ purchases across the chain in event window $t$, and let $\mathcal{H}_{iet}^{-}$ denote the products the consumer purchased anywhere in the chain before that window. We define novelty-seeking as
\begin{equation}
	\text{Y}^{N}_{iet}
	= \frac{\left|\left\{j\in\mathcal{J}_{iet}:j\notin\mathcal{H}_{iet}^{-}\right\}\right|}
	{\left|\mathcal{J}_{iet}\right|}.
\end{equation}
Each product counts once within a window, regardless of how many times the consumer purchases it. A higher value therefore indicates that a larger share of the consumer's distinct product set is new to that consumer. Novelty-seeking is defined only when a consumer purchases at least one product in the window; otherwise the denominator is zero and the outcome is missing. It therefore describes product composition conditional on purchasing, whereas purchase frequency captures the extensive margin. Appendix~\ref{app:new_product_seeking} uses new-product-seeking as an alternative outcome definition based on products that have appeared recently in the transaction data.

Having defined the outcomes and predicted purchase-incidence status, we next plot the raw variation behind the triple-differences design. Figure~\ref{fig:novelty_intention_paths} shows customer-weighted mean novelty-seeking and closure-clustered confidence intervals for the four estimation cells, omitting the closure window. In the low-predicted-incidence panel, treated and control means are similar before closure, and the treated path is relatively higher in the first post-reopening windows. In the high-predicted-incidence panel, treated episodes begin at a higher level, but the treated-control gap narrows after reopening. The DDD below compares these changes rather than interpreting either level difference as causal.

These paths are descriptive for an additional reason: novelty-seeking is defined only in windows with a purchase, and the number of purchasing windows varies across cells and relative periods. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_purchase_probability} displays the realized probability of at least one purchase in each window. These purchase probabilities determine which windows enter the novelty-seeking regression. The figure here provides intuition for the comparison; the coefficient estimates and event-study diagnostics provide the inferential evidence.

\subsection{Triple Difference-in-Differences}
\label{section:3.3}  

\noindent To estimate the incremental response associated with interrupting a likely purchase, we compare (i) treated versus control customers, (ii) behavior before and after reopening, and (iii) high versus low predicted purchase incidence.

The logic follows the canonical triple-differences design in \shortciteA{Gruber1994}, who studies group-specific mandates by comparing affected and unaffected groups across states with and without policy changes before and after adoption. In that setting, the third difference removes shocks common to adopting states, shocks common to the affected group, and aggregate time effects, leaving the component specific to the treated affected group. In our setting, closure exposure is the treated-unit dimension, predicted purchase incidence is the affected-group dimension, and the post-reopening period is the time dimension. Recent applications use the same idea in richer interaction settings. \shortciteA{gehring2026insuring}, for example, interpret the interaction between drought exposure and insurance rollout as a triple-difference-style estimator with continuous treatment dimensions and emphasize that the main threat is an omitted factor that moves with the interaction term. More closely, \shortciteA{Levine2025} study the impact of temporary store closure on consumers who likely planned a visit during the closure, focusing on visiting frequency later.
This perspective motivates the coefficient decomposition and pretrend tests below. 

\noindent Formally, we estimate the following pooled specification:
\begin{equation}
	Y_{iet} = \delta^B \cdot \text{Post} \times \text{Treated}
	+ \beta \cdot \text{Post} \times \text{High}
	+ \delta^D \cdot \text{Post} \times \text{Treated} \times \text{High}
	+ \phi_{ie} + \omega_t + \gamma_m + \varepsilon_{iet},
	\label{eq:main_ddd}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{iet}$ denotes the outcome of interest for member $i$ in period $t$ of event $e$. The indicator $\text{Post}$ captures periods after reopening, $\text{Treated}$ identifies customers exposed to a store closure, and $\text{High}$ identifies episodes with high predicted purchase incidence during the closure window. This indicator is defined ex ante for both treated and control member-events; only treated high-predicted-incidence episodes face a likely interrupted purchase. The specification includes member-closure fixed effects $\phi_{ie}$, relative-period fixed effects $\omega_t$ and calendar-month fixed effects $\gamma_m$. Because treatment exposure is generated by a common store-closure shock, standard errors are clustered by closure event. The main specification therefore uses 18 clusters.

The pooled design stacks the 18 closure events. In the retained sample, each customer appears in exactly one event, and no treated store is used as a control store. Closure windows can overlap in calendar time; calendar-month fixed effects absorb common monthly conditions, but do not make events equally weighted. The pooled coefficient is implicitly weighted by the customer-period observations contributed by each event, which range with the number of eligible customers. These features are part of the interpretation of the pooled estimand rather than evidence that each closure has a separately estimated effect.

The low-predicted-incidence treatment effect is $\delta^B$. The high-predicted-incidence treatment effect is $\delta^B+\delta^D$. Thus $\delta^D$ is the high-minus-low treatment-effect differential, not the total effect for the high group. The parameter $\beta$ is a high-minus-low post shift common to treated and control episodes; it is not a treatment effect.

\subsubsection{Identification Assumptions and Diagnostic Tests}
\label{section:3.3.1} 

\noindent The causal interpretation of the high-minus-low differential requires two central assumptions. First, parallel triple trends must hold: absent the closure, the treated-control gap for high-predicted-incidence episodes would have evolved like the corresponding gap for low-predicted-incidence episodes.

Second, we require \textit{comparable general closure effects}. A closure may affect customers for reasons other than interrupting a likely purchase---for example, by changing convenience, promotions, substitution opportunities or perceptions of the store. The low-predicted-incidence comparison captures these general effects. The high-predicted-incidence comparison contains the same general effects plus the consequence of a likely interrupted purchase. If general closure effects would otherwise be comparable across the two groups, subtracting the low-group response from the high-group response isolates the incremental response associated with the interruption.

Implementation additionally requires an ex-ante classifier and an informative control path. The classifier uses only pre-closure behavior, and the matched control stores remain open during the focal closure. Closure timing is not assigned to individual customers and is plausibly external to their product choices, but the data do not record each closure's cause or announcement date. We therefore do not rule out unobserved local operational shocks. Under parallel triple trends and comparable general closure effects, $\delta^D$ identifies the incremental response associated with interrupting a likely purchase.

We assess these assumptions in the same order. First, Table~\ref{tab:treatment_control_balance} compares treated and control episodes within each predicted-incidence group. The low-incidence cells have similar pre-period outcomes, while the high-incidence cells differ more in pre-period novelty and closure duration. These level differences motivate the fixed effects and group-specific pretrend checks below.

Second, Figure~\ref{fig:closure_shock_purchase_frequency} verifies the first-stage shock. Purchase frequency falls sharply for treated customers during the closure window, while the control path remains available. This confirms that the closures create the intended local interruption while keeping the product-choice outcome conceptually separate from purchase incidence.

Third, we test pre-period trends using the event-study version of the triple-difference specification. With closure-clustered inference, the purchase-frequency high-minus-low pretrend rejects equality ($p=0.035$), while its continuous-score counterpart is borderline ($p=0.050$). We therefore treat the positive purchase-frequency estimate as supporting evidence of recovery, not as the paper's cleanest causal result. For novelty-seeking, Appendix~\ref{app:identification_diagnostics} reports the three pre-period DDD coefficients, their confidence intervals and the joint test that they equal zero (Wald statistic $=0.837$, $p=0.841$). The appendix also plots the complete coefficient path and reports a common-support restriction. The matched estimates are smaller and imprecise, so this diagnostic tempers rather than strengthens the identifying case.

\paragraph{Purchase frequency.}
The low-predicted-incidence treated-control post contrast is 0.09 percentage points per calendar day (SE 0.24; $p=0.711$). The high-group contrast is 0.67 points (SE 0.45; $p=0.152$), or 13.6\% of the pre-period mean. Their difference is 0.58 points per day (SE 0.32; $p=0.086$), about 11.8\% of the pre-period mean. The point estimates describe a relative post-reopening rebound, but none of the group-specific contrasts is statistically distinguishable from zero at the 5\% level under closure-clustered inference. Together with the rejected high-minus-low purchase pretrend, this evidence is descriptive rather than a clean estimate of an interruption-induced recovery.

\paragraph{Novelty-seeking.}
For low-predicted-incidence episodes, the treated-control contrast is a 3.13-percentage-point increase in novelty-seeking (SE 1.78; $p=0.098$). The high-group effect is a 1.02-point decline (SE 0.72; $p=0.175$). Neither group-specific contrast is statistically distinguishable from zero at the 5\% level. Their difference is $-4.15$ points (SE 1.84; $p=0.038$), or 8.3\% of the pre-period outcome mean. In economic terms, the differential more than offsets the 3.13-point low-group increase, leaving the high-group point estimate slightly negative. Under parallel triple trends and comparable general closure effects, this pattern indicates that interrupting a likely purchase offsets the relative increase in exploration observed for the low group. It does not establish a precisely estimated absolute decline for the high group.

Appendix~\ref{app:new_product_seeking} checks whether this conclusion depends on defining novelty relative to the consumer's own history. The robustness analysis instead measures new-product-seeking using products that have appeared recently in the transaction data. Its estimates show the same qualitative reduction in exploration after a likely purchase is interrupted. Section~\ref{section:5.1.1} examines whether the novelty response varies with consumers' baseline orientation toward familiar versus novel products.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{section:4.1}

We further assess robustness by replacing the binary high-predicted-incidence indicator with the centered ex-ante predicted purchase-incidence score. The treatment-by-score interaction is positive for purchase frequency ($+0.0076$, SE $0.0051$; $p=0.152$) and negative for novelty-seeking ($-0.0452$, SE $0.0276$; $p=0.120$). A 10-percentage-point increase in predicted purchase incidence changes the fitted treated-control post contrast by approximately $+0.08$ percentage points per day for purchase frequency and $-0.45$ points for novelty-seeking. The signs match the binary DDDs, but the continuous-score estimates are imprecise under closure-clustered inference. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix_score_main_results} reports the complete specification. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix_new_product_seeking} reports the corresponding binary and continuous estimates for new-product-seeking.

\section{Heterogeneity by Baseline Novelty-Seeking}
\label{section:5.1.1} 

\noindent Consumers with lower baseline novelty-seeking reveal a stronger orientation toward familiar products and established routines. When an expected purchase is interrupted, the salient counterfactual may therefore be more focused on the familiar product that would otherwise have been chosen. If our mechanism holds, the interruption should reduce novelty-seeking more strongly among these consumers.

\paragraph{Trait construction and distribution.}
For each customer-closure episode, we first calculate novelty-seeking separately in relative periods $-8$ through $-1$ and then take the unweighted mean across periods in which the customer purchased. Each relative period has the length of its closure event, so these are eight closure-length windows rather than eight calendar weeks. We define the episode's \emph{baseline orders}, $O_{ie}$, as the total number of distinct orders that customer $i$ placed across all chain stores observed in the market during these same eight windows. An order is a distinct transaction, not a purchased product or a purchasing day. We require $O_{ie}\geq5$ to reduce the influence of very short purchase histories. The raw novelty measure ranges from zero, when every purchased product in these windows is familiar, to one, when every distinct product purchased in each purchasing window is new relative to the customer's earlier history.

Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_novelty_distribution} shows the resulting distribution across 18,525 eligible episodes. The mean is 0.508, the standard deviation is 0.269 and the median is 0.500. The 10th and 90th percentiles are 0.167 and 0.889; 3.3\% of episodes are at zero and 6.8\% are at one. Thus, the analysis uses broad variation over the full support rather than a comparison driven by a narrow part of the distribution. Among the 17,866 episodes with an observed novelty outcome, every treatment-by-predicted-incidence cell contains all 18 closures; cell sizes range from 1,489 to 7,951 episodes, and cell means range from 0.453 to 0.574.

The raw data also show why the amount of baseline purchase history should be separated from baseline novelty. Across the 18,525 eligible episodes, the correlation between baseline novelty and $\log(1+O_{ie})$ is $-0.329$. Mean baseline novelty is 0.620 among episodes with exactly five orders and 0.365 among episodes with at least 20 orders; the share at the upper boundary of one falls from 19.3\% to 0.1\% across these groups. This model-free association may reflect both genuine differences between frequent and infrequent customers and the greater sampling variability of novelty measured from few orders. Either source can make order history independently relevant for heterogeneity in the post-period response.

\paragraph{Econometric specification.}
Let $N_{ie}$ denote standardized baseline novelty-seeking. We transform the baseline-order count as $Q_{ie}=[\log(1+O_{ie})-\overline{\log(1+O)}]/\operatorname{SD}[\log(1+O)]$. The logarithm compresses the right tail of the order-count distribution, and standardization gives $Q_{ie}$ mean zero and unit standard deviation. Retaining the Post, Treated and High definitions from equation~\eqref{eq:main_ddd}, our preferred specification is
\begin{align}
Y^N_{iet}
={}& \delta^B(\text{Post}\times\text{Treated})
+\beta(\text{Post}\times\text{High})
+\delta^D(\text{Post}\times\text{Treated}\times\text{High}) \notag\\
&+\eta_0(\text{Post}\times N_{ie})
+\eta_T(\text{Post}\times\text{Treated}\times N_{ie}) \notag\\
&+\eta_H(\text{Post}\times\text{High}\times N_{ie})
+\theta^D(\text{Post}\times\text{Treated}\times\text{High}\times N_{ie}) \notag\\
&+\text{Post}\times Q_{ie}
\left(\kappa_0+\kappa_T\text{Treated}+\kappa_H\text{High}
+\kappa_D\text{Treated}\times\text{High}\right) \notag\\
&+\phi_{ie}+\omega_t+\gamma_m+\varepsilon_{iet},
\label{eq:baseline_novelty_heterogeneity}
\end{align}
where $Y^N_{iet}$ is novelty-seeking conditional on purchase. The first line is exactly the main DDD specification for this outcome. Member-closure fixed effects $\phi_{ie}$ absorb all time-invariant episode characteristics, $\omega_t$ are relative-period fixed effects and $\gamma_m$ are calendar-month fixed effects. The next two lines add the complete Post-by-baseline-novelty interaction hierarchy.

The line involving $Q_{ie}$ allows post-period changes to vary flexibly with the amount of baseline purchase history. In the control, low-predicted-incidence cell, $\kappa_0$ is the change in the post-period outcome gradient associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in log baseline orders. The coefficients $\kappa_T$ and $\kappa_H$ allow that gradient to differ for treated and high-predicted-incidence episodes, respectively, while $\kappa_D$ allows the high-minus-low DDD itself to vary with baseline orders. The levels of $Q_{ie}$ and its group interactions are time-invariant and therefore absorbed by $\phi_{ie}$; their interactions with Post are the estimable terms. This complete hierarchy prevents $\theta^D$ from capturing heterogeneity associated with how many orders were available to measure baseline novelty. Because $N_{ie}$ and $Q_{ie}$ are standardized, $\delta^D$ is the high-minus-low DDD at mean baseline novelty and mean baseline order history. The parameter $\theta^D$ is the change in this DDD for a one-standard-deviation increase in baseline novelty, holding baseline order history fixed. We also estimate an otherwise identical specification after deleting the entire line involving $Q_{ie}$. Both specifications use the same sample, outcome, fixed effects and closure-event clustering.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:baseline_novelty_heterogeneity} reports the adjusted and unadjusted estimates side by side. With the baseline-order hierarchy, a one-standard-deviation increase in baseline novelty raises the DDD by 2.94 percentage points (SE $=1.47$; one-sided $p=0.031$). Without that hierarchy, the gradient remains positive at 2.29 points (SE $=1.43$; one-sided $p=0.064$). The direction and approximate magnitude therefore do not depend on the baseline-order adjustment, although the unadjusted estimate does not meet the 5\% one-sided threshold. We prefer the adjusted specification because the raw association above shows that measured baseline novelty varies sharply with the number of orders used to construct it.

One standard deviation is 0.269 on the raw baseline-novelty scale. In the adjusted specification, the DDD at mean baseline novelty and order history is $-1.30$ points (SE $=2.00$). Holding order history at its mean, the linear fit moves from a $-4.24$-point DDD at raw baseline novelty of about 0.240---one standard deviation below the mean---to a $+1.63$-point DDD at raw baseline novelty of about 0.777---one standard deviation above the mean. This two-standard-deviation movement changes the fitted interruption response by 5.88 points. These fitted contrasts summarize the adjusted slope; they are not separately estimated subgroup effects.

\subsection{Mechanism 2: Missed Exposure to New Products}
\label{section:5.2} 

\noindent A second potential explanation for the decline in novelty-seeking is that treated customers are less exposed to new products during the closure period. While control customers continue to visit open stores and observe evolving assortments, treated customers are unable to interact with the store while it is closed and may therefore miss newly introduced items. If this mechanism is operative, treated customers should return after reopening with lower awareness of the updated product assortment and may therefore be less likely to try new products. In contrast to the goal-based mechanism, which operates through the persistence of purchase intentions, this channel operates through differences in information and exposure.

\subsubsection{Implementation and Findings}
\label{section:5.2.1} 

\noindent To evaluate this mechanism, we construct a measure of product introduction intensity using matched control stores that remain open during each closure event. For each closure $e$, we first identify the set of matched control stores that are comparable to the treated store but remain open during the closure window. Second, within each control store, we count the number of products sold during the closure window that were not sold in the immediately preceding window of equal length. This captures the introduction of new products over the closure period. Then, we average this measure across matched control stores to obtain a closure-level measure of introduction intensity. Finally, we estimate the key triple difference-in-differences coefficient separately for each closure event and relate the estimated novelty effect to the corresponding introduction intensity.

Across the 18 closure events in the data, matched control stores introduce on average 9.3 new products during the closure window, with substantial variation across events, ranging from 1.8 to 15.4 new products. This variation provides a natural source of cross-event heterogeneity to test the missed exposure mechanism.

If missed exposure were the dominant driver of the decline in novelty-seeking, closures with greater product introduction intensity should exhibit more negative novelty effects. That is, the reduction in exploration should be strongest when treated customers miss more new product introductions.

The evidence indicates that missed exposure is unlikely to be the main mechanism behind the observed decline in novelty-seeking. Instead, the results are more consistent with the goal-based mechanism, whereby the interruption of an active purchase leads to more focused behavior upon return. This interpretation is supported both by the lack of correlation between introduction intensity and novelty effects and by the persistence of the main results across alternative specifications.

\section{Behavioral Mechanisms and Alternative Explanations}
\label{section:7A}

\noindent The novelty-seeking DDD is consistent with familiar-choice salience, but it does not by itself identify a psychological mechanism. We therefore assess alternative explanations by asking whether each requires an observable intermediate response that is present in the data. The first test considers a supply-side explanation: stores may reopen with fewer products or an assortment tilted toward familiar items.

\subsection{Reopening Assortment Constraints}
\label{section:alternative_assortment}

A narrower menu after reopening could reduce product exploration without changing consumer preferences. For this explanation to account for the DDD, two links must hold. First, treated stores must reopen with a smaller or less stable assortment than matched control stores. Second, high-predicted-incidence customers must be more exposed or more responsive to that contraction than low-predicted-incidence customers. A store-level contraction common to all treated customers cannot by itself generate the high-minus-low treatment differential. We test the necessary first link directly.

We construct a store-week panel for the 18 closures and their 90 matched control stores, using four seven-day periods before closure and four after reopening. Because transaction volume changes around closures, a raw product count could mistake fewer purchases for a smaller menu. We therefore use three complementary measures of realized assortment: the share of core pre-closure products that continue to appear, the number of distinct products in a standardized sample of 50 customer-day baskets and the overlap between each week's products and the store's pre-closure product set. Appendix~\ref{app:reopening_assortment} describes the measures and matched-store specification.

We find no evidence of a relative contraction at reopening. Core-product coverage falls by 1.29 percentage points more at treated stores than at controls (95\% CI: $-8.47$ to $5.90$ percentage points). Standardized product breadth instead rises by 0.31 products per 50 baskets (95\% CI: $-0.79$ to $1.40$), while overlap with the pre-closure menu rises by 1.46 percentage points (95\% CI: $-3.81$ to $6.74$ percentage points). The breadth interval rules out a relative loss larger than 0.79 products per 50 baskets, or 2.8\% of the control pre-period mean. All three immediate-reopening estimates are positive, and none of the joint pretrend tests rejects equality of the pre-period coefficients. The estimates are also similar when we require a complete eight-period store panel.

We obtain the same conclusion from cross-closure variation. If assortment constraints drive the novelty result, closures with larger early declines in treated-store assortment should exhibit more negative novelty DDDs. The estimated relationships are close to zero across all three measures; permutation $p$-values range from 0.62 to 0.96. These results do not show that the formal catalogs were identical---we observe products sold, not the menu displayed to every customer. Within that measurement limit, however, we do not detect the store-level first stage required by the assortment-constraint explanation.

\subsection{New-Product Notifications}
\label{section:alternative_new_product_notifications}

Inactivity-triggered marketing provides a second alternative explanation. Low-predicted-incidence customers are more likely to remain inactive long enough to receive new-product notifications. If the closure or reopening increases this exposure disproportionately for treated low-incidence customers, the notifications could raise their exploration and make the high-minus-low novelty DDD negative even without a narrowing of choice among treated high-incidence customers. This explanation therefore requires a negative DDD in new-product-notification exposure: the treated high-minus-low exposure gap must fall during the closure or after reopening relative to its pre-closure and control-group counterparts.

We identify new-product notifications from the platform's campaign classification and count each member-policy-date combination once. The primary outcome is the number of such campaigns per consumer-day. Absolute exposure is higher for treated low-incidence customers, consistent with the inactivity trigger. Their mean rate is 0.0247 per day before closure, 0.0307 during closure and 0.0250 after reopening. The corresponding rates for treated high-incidence customers are 0.0058, 0.0115 and 0.0113. These level differences cannot explain the novelty DDD because they largely predate the closure. We therefore estimate the same treated-versus-control, high-versus-low differential for notification exposure. Appendix~\ref{app:new_product_notifications} describes the construction, supporting outcomes and inference.

The differential exposure moves in the opposite direction from the alternative explanation. During closure, the high-minus-low treated-control change is $+0.0041$ new-product campaigns per consumer-day (95\% CI: $0.0019$ to $0.0064$; restricted wild-cluster $p=0.003$). After reopening, it remains positive at $+0.0017$ per day (95\% CI: $0.0001$ to $0.0032$; restricted wild-cluster $p=0.047$). The event-study leads do not reject a common pretrend ($p=0.500$). Extensive-margin estimates yield the same pattern: the relative probability of receiving any new-product notification rises by 4.22 percentage points during closure and 1.44 points after reopening. The share of all campaigns devoted to new products does not change differentially, so the exposure result reflects campaign volume rather than a shift in content composition.

Under the natural assumption that new-product notifications weakly increase product awareness or experimentation, this exposure pattern would work against the negative novelty DDD rather than generate it. The evidence therefore does not support differential recorded new-product notifications as the explanation for the product-choice result. This test is narrower than a complete audit of the recommendation system: campaign records identify targeting rather than verified impressions, and they do not observe personalized rankings or other in-app recommendations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{section:7}

\noindent This paper studies how consumers respond when a temporary store closure is likely to interrupt a purchase. Using panel data from a large digital coffee chain, we estimate a triple-differences design that compares high- and low-predicted-incidence post-reopening responses. Purchase frequency is 0.58 percentage points per day higher for high- than for low-predicted-incidence episodes after reopening. Because the corresponding pretrend test rejects equality, this estimate is descriptive evidence of recovery rather than a clean causal rebound.

The product-choice result is more credible under the design's pretrend diagnostics. Novelty-seeking responds 4.15 percentage points less for high- than for low-predicted-incidence episodes. The direct high-group effect is a statistically insignificant 1.02-point decline. Under parallel triple trends and comparable general closure effects, this DDD identifies the incremental response associated with interrupting a likely purchase. An appendix robustness check based on new-product-seeking yields the same qualitative conclusion when exploration is defined relative to products that have appeared recently in the transaction data. Consistent with familiar-choice salience, the interruption-related narrowing of product exploration is stronger among consumers with lower baseline novelty-seeking.

A goal-persistence mechanism is one interpretation: an interrupted likely purchase may make familiar products more salient upon return. The main DDD does not isolate this psychological channel from other reasons that an interruption may reduce exploration.

Our findings contribute to the marketing literature in several ways. First, we show how a temporary constraint interacts with ex-ante purchase incidence to shape post-reopening behavior; persistent purchase goals are one possible account of the product-choice response rather than a separately identified mechanism. Second, we show that supply-side shocks can have distinct effects on the level and composition of demand, and that these effects depend critically on whether the constraint is likely to bind. By focusing on consumers for whom the closure likely interrupts a purchase, we uncover behavioral responses that can be masked in aggregate analyses. Furthermore, our study contributes to a broader understanding of product discovery by showing how temporary interruptions can change consumer choice even when demand levels recover quickly.

From a managerial perspective, purchase-day frequency can recover while product discovery weakens. The novelty-seeking estimate is a relative effect, and the alternative new-product-seeking definition strengthens the evidence that exploration narrows after a likely purchase is interrupted. Firms should therefore monitor product discovery alongside post-disruption sales recovery. More generally, demand recovery is incomplete when customers return but narrow the set of products they consider.

\section{Data Construction and Measurement}
\label{app:data_construction}

\noindent This appendix records construction details that are needed to interpret the empirical design but are too mechanical for the main text. The unit of assignment is a customer-closure episode. A customer enters through a closure event for which her pre-closure purchase history identifies a meaningful focal store; the outcomes then aggregate her purchases across the observed chain.

\subsection{Closure Registry and Customer-Episode Sample}
\label{app:closure_registry}

The raw data contain 7,312,498 orders, represented by 10,631,250 product-line records, from 779,744 customers at 260 stores between June 1, 2020 and December 31, 2021. We aggregate these orders to a store-day panel and define a candidate closure as a consecutive spell in which a store records no transactions. This procedure identifies 101 zero-transaction spells.

We first remove 60 spells at stores located in universities or colleges. These spells occur during summer periods in which COVID-19 policies may have kept students away from campus. Because the data cover purchases made in this city, we do not observe whether students continued to buy from the chain elsewhere. A campus store with no local transactions may therefore reflect the temporary absence of its customers rather than a supply disruption. Removing these spells avoids treating a seasonal loss of the local customer population as a store closure and leaves 41 non-campus candidates.

We next exclude seven spells lasting 30 days or longer. The analysis is designed around short disruptions followed by a clearly observed reopening; longer spells are more likely to reflect seasonal shutdowns or persistent changes in store operations. They also produce much longer pre- and post-event windows, which are difficult to compare within the 19-month data period. Requiring closures to last strictly fewer than 30 days leaves 34 candidates.

The remaining events must provide enough support for the treated-control comparison. We require at least one treated and one control customer and at least 50 customers in each group. These restrictions remove events for which the DDD is undefined or rests on a very small group. We also require the control purchase rate during the closure to be at least twice the treated rate. This screen is intended to distinguish a localized access disruption from a broader contraction in demand that affects treated and control customers alike. Together, the support and purchase-rate screens remove 12 events---eight for a low control purchase rate, two for insufficient group size and two for having no control customers---leaving 22 closures. Because the purchase-rate screen uses behavior realized during the closure, the full-registry sensitivity analysis remains important.

The maintained headline sample excludes four additional events around the Lunar New Year period. Travel, holiday schedules and COVID-19-related mobility changes during this period can shift customer traffic independently of a store closure, and these events have weaker treatment-control fit. This restriction leaves 18 closures lasting 10 to 29 days. It is not generated by a coded ex-ante rule, so we retain the 22-event full-registry analysis as a sensitivity check. The novelty DDD keeps its sign in that broader registry, while the purchase-frequency estimate is less stable across broader sample definitions; the archived sensitivity analysis records those comparisons without changing the 18-event headline registry.

For each retained closure, treated customers are those whose preferred pre-closure store is the store that closes. Control customers are those whose preferred store is one of the matched stores that remains open. We define the preferred store from pre-closure purchases and require at least five pre-closure purchase days and a preferred-store purchase share of at least 80\%. These restrictions ensure that the closing store was an economically meaningful source of access for treated customers. The final sample contains 40,148 customer-closure episodes: 7,390 treated and 32,758 controls. Of the treated episodes, 2,041, or 27.6\%, have high predicted purchase incidence during the closure window.

\section{Identification Diagnostics}
\label{app:identification_diagnostics}

\noindent This appendix reports the formal diagnostics behind the identification discussion in Section~\ref{section:3.3.1}. The main design has three dimensions: treatment exposure, post-reopening timing and predicted purchase incidence. The key identifying restriction is therefore a triple-difference restriction: absent the closure, the treated-control gap for high-predicted-incidence episodes would have changed like the corresponding gap for low-predicted-incidence episodes.

\subsection{Pretrend Tests}
\label{app:pretrend_tests}

For the main novelty-seeking result, we estimate the event-study analog of equation~\eqref{eq:main_ddd}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Y^N_{iet}={}&\alpha_{ie}+\lambda_t+\gamma_m
+\sum_{\ell\in\{-4,-3,-2,1,2,3,4\}}
\Big[
\delta^B_{\ell}\,\text{Treated}_{ie}
+\beta_{\ell}\,\text{High}_{ie} \\
&\hspace{37mm}
+\delta^D_{\ell}\big(\text{Treated}_{ie}\times\text{High}_{ie}\big)
\Big]\mathbb{1}\{t=\ell\}+\varepsilon_{iet}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:novelty_event_study}
\end{equation}
Period $-1$ is the omitted reference period, and period 0 is the closure window and is excluded. The coefficient $\delta^D_{\ell}$ is the high-minus-low DDD at relative period $\ell$. The pretrend test is the joint null
$H_0:\delta^D_{-4}=\delta^D_{-3}=\delta^D_{-2}=0$.

\subsection{Reopening Assortment Constraints}
\label{app:reopening_assortment}

This section reports the construction and estimates behind the assortment test in Section~\ref{section:alternative_assortment}. The analysis begins with the 18 treated stores and their five matched control stores. For each closure, we form four fixed seven-day periods ending immediately before closure and four fixed seven-day periods beginning immediately after reopening. Fixed weeks keep the assortment window comparable across events even though the closures last 10 to 29 days.

We construct three measures from product-level transactions. \textit{Core-product coverage} first defines a store's core set as products sold in at least three of the four pre-closure weeks, then measures the share of that set sold in each week. \textit{Products per 50 baskets} draws 50 customer-day baskets without replacement, counts their distinct products and averages this count over 100 draws. This rarefaction holds observed basket volume fixed; store-weeks with fewer than 50 baskets are missing. \textit{Pre-menu overlap} is the Jaccard similarity between the products sold in a store-week and the union of products sold in that store's four pre-closure weeks.

For assortment outcome $A_{est}$ at store $s$, event $e$ and relative week $t$, we estimate
\begin{equation}
	A_{est}=\beta\big(\text{TreatedStore}_{es}\times\text{Post}_{et}\big)
	+\alpha_{es}+\lambda_{et}+\varepsilon_{est},
	\label{eq:assortment_did}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{es}$ denotes event-store fixed effects and $\lambda_{et}$ denotes event-by-relative-week fixed effects. The latter compare the treated store with its matched controls within the same event and week. Standard errors are clustered by closure event. We also replace Post with relative-week indicators, omitting week $-1$, to estimate the immediate reopening contrast and test the three pre-period coefficients jointly.

\subsection{New-Product Notification Exposure}
\label{app:new_product_notifications}

This section reports the construction and estimates behind Section~\ref{section:alternative_new_product_notifications}. The raw files contain 56,724,521 push records. We retain the 40,148 consumers in the 18-closure analysis and map notifications into the four event-length periods before closure, the closure window and the four event-length periods after reopening. This produces a balanced panel of 361,332 member-event-period observations. Of the 912,911 push records mapped into these windows, 125,918 have \texttt{trigger\_tag}=3, the platform classification for a new-product notification.

The primary measure counts unique member-policy-date notification campaigns and divides by the number of calendar days in the event window. All 125,918 new-product records are unique at this grain, so the campaign and raw-record measures coincide. Supporting outcomes indicate whether a member receives any new-product notification, count distinct notification days and measure the share of all campaigns classified as new-product notifications. The source files record targeting entries; they do not verify delivery, impression, opening or reading.

For exposure outcome $N_{iet}$, we estimate
\begin{equation}
	N_{iet}=\beta_1(\text{After}_{et}\times\text{Treated}_{i})
	+\beta_2(\text{After}_{et}\times\text{High}_{i})
	+\beta_3(\text{After}_{et}\times\text{Treated}_{i}\times\text{High}_{i})
	+\phi_{ie}+\omega_t+\gamma_m+\varepsilon_{iet},
	\label{eq:new_product_notification_ddd}
\end{equation}
where $\text{After}_{et}$ denotes either the closure window or the post-reopening periods. The closure specification compares period 0 with all four pre-periods; a sensitivity check compares it only with period $-1$. The post specification omits period 0 and compares the four post-periods with the four pre-periods, as in the main outcome regression. Standard errors are clustered by closure event. Because there are 18 clusters, Table~\ref{tab:appendix_new_product_notifications} also reports restricted wild-cluster $p$-values for $\beta_3$.

The inactivity pattern in the records also clarifies why absolute high-low levels are not informative. Among treated consumers, the median new-product notification occurs 121 days after the last purchase for low-incidence episodes before closure and 129 days during closure. The corresponding medians for high-incidence episodes are 56 and 40 days. Low-incidence consumers therefore receive more notifications in levels because they are ordinarily more dormant. The DDD removes this persistent gap and shows that the relative exposure increase occurs among treated high-incidence consumers instead.

\subsubsection{Continuous predicted-incidence score}

To avoid relying on the 0.5 classification threshold, we replace $\text{High}_{ie}$ with the mean-centered predicted purchase-incidence score $\widetilde{s}_{ie}$:
\begin{equation}
	Y_{iet} = \delta_0^B \cdot \text{Post} \times \text{Treated}
	+ \beta^S \cdot \text{Post} \times \widetilde{s}_{ie}
	+ \delta^S \cdot \text{Post} \times \text{Treated} \times \widetilde{s}_{ie}
	+ \phi_{ie} + \omega_t + \gamma_m + \varepsilon_{iet}.
	\label{eq:score_ddd}
\end{equation}
Because the score is centered, $\delta_0^B$ is the treated-control post contrast at the mean predicted incidence. The coefficient $\beta^S$ is the common post-score gradient, while $\delta^S$ is the change in the treated-control post contrast associated with a one-unit increase in predicted purchase incidence.

The continuous estimates preserve the binary results' sign pattern. Higher predicted purchase incidence is associated with a larger treated-control recovery in purchase frequency and a smaller treated-control post response in novelty-seeking. \subsection{Alternative Outcome Definition: New-Product-Seeking}
\label{app:new_product_seeking}

\noindent The main novelty-seeking outcome defines exploration relative to the customer's own purchase history. As a robustness check, we instead ask whether customers select products that have appeared recently in the transaction data. Let $F_j$ be product $j$'s first observed sale date and let $\mathcal{W}_{et}$ and $\mathcal{W}_{e,t-1}$ be the current and immediately previous event windows. We define new-product-seeking as
\begin{equation}
	\text{Y}^{NP}_{iet}
	= \frac{\left|\left\{j\in\mathcal{J}_{iet}:F_j\in
	\mathcal{W}_{e,t-1}\cup\mathcal{W}_{et}\right\}\right|}
	{\left|\mathcal{J}_{iet}\right|}.
\end{equation}
This measure is the share of distinct products purchased in the current window whose first observed sale falls in the current or immediately previous event window. It proxies for recently introduced products, but first observed sale need not be the formal catalog launch date. Like novelty-seeking, it is defined only in windows with a purchase.

The binary specification yields a 0.80-point low-incidence effect and a 3.37-point decline for high-predicted-incidence episodes. Their difference is $-4.17$ percentage points (SE 0.89; $p<0.001$), or 28.6\% of the pre-period mean. The continuous-score specification leads to the same conclusion: a 10-percentage-point increase in predicted purchase incidence lowers the treated-control post contrast by 0.49 percentage points ($p=0.006$). The high-minus-low pretrend tests do not reject for either specification ($p=0.114$ for the binary measure and $p=0.377$ for the continuous score). The robustness check therefore supports the main novelty-seeking result using a definition tied to recently appearing products rather than the customer's own history.
